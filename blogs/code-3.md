# 1. Introduction

Up to this point in **Code**, we have learned that computers are not mysterious machines—they are built upon surprisingly simple ideas.

We started with communication using **Morse Code** and **Braille**.

Then we learned that **electricity is simply the movement of electrons**.

Next, we discovered that **switches create binary states (ON and OFF)**, forming the basis of every digital system.

Now Charles Petzold asks a completely new question:

> **What if two people want to communicate, but they can't see each other anymore?**

This seemingly simple problem becomes the birth of **modern telecommunications**.

---

## Hidden Lesson

Petzold is no longer teaching electricity for its own sake.

Instead, he begins showing how **electricity can carry information across distance**.

This chapter marks the transition from:

```text
Electrical Engineering

↓

```

Electricity is no longer used merely to light a bulb—

To allow replies,

```text

Friend ───────► You
```

Now both people can:

- Send
- Receive

This is called **bidirectional communication**.

Modern examples include:

- Telephone
- Internet
- Bluetooth
- Wi-Fi
- USB
- Computer Networks

Almost every modern communication system works this way.

---

## Why This Matters

True communication requires both:

- Transmission
- Reception

Modern networking,

Without replies,

there is no conversation—

only broadcasting.

### Loop 2

These loops never interfere with each other.

## Engineering Insight

Although the communication system appears to be shared,

This isolation allows both communication channels to operate independently,

laying the foundation for modern networking systems.

The amount of copper required would become enormous.

Engineers naturally asked:

> **Can we transmit the same information using fewer wires?**

The answer was **yes**.

## Hidden Lesson

Engineering is not only about making things work.

- Cheaper

- More efficient

# 12. The Common Wire

Engineers noticed something interesting.

Both communication circuits require a **return path**.

Instead of giving each circuit its own return wire,

both circuits can safely **share one conductor**.

The new arrangement becomes:

```text
Signal Wire A

Signal Wire B

Shared Return Wire
```

Instead of:

```text
4 Wires
```

the system now requires only:

```text
3 Wires
```

This shared return conductor is called the **Common**.

---

## Why This Matters

The savings may seem small,

but for telegraph lines stretching hundreds or thousands of kilometers,

eliminating one entire wire saved:

- Large amounts of copper
- Installation costs
- Maintenance costs
- Construction time

A **25% reduction** in wiring represented a major engineering achievement.

---

## Engineering Insight

This is one of the earliest examples of **resource sharing**.

Instead of duplicating infrastructure,

multiple systems share part of it without affecting one another.

This idea later appears throughout computer engineering.

---

# 13. Why Doesn't the Shared Wire Cause Problems?

This is where many beginners become confused.

If both circuits share one wire,

why don't the electrical currents mix together?

The answer lies in understanding **closed loops**.

Current from **Battery A** always returns to **Battery A**.

Current from **Battery B** always returns to **Battery B**.

Although both circuits use the same physical conductor,

their electrical loops remain independent.

The **wire is shared**.

The **circuits are not**.

---

## Visualizing the Idea

Think of two different buses using the same road.

```text
Bus A

────────────►

Shared Road

◄────────────

Bus B
```

The road is shared,

but each bus still follows its own route.

Likewise,

electrical current follows its own complete circuit.

---

## Computer Science Connection

Modern computers constantly share hardware resources.

Examples include:

- Memory buses
- PCI Express lanes
- USB buses
- Ethernet cables
- Shared storage systems

Multiple devices may share the same physical infrastructure,

yet each communication remains logically independent.

This same engineering principle first appeared in telegraph circuits.

---

# 14. The Engineering Principle

This introduces one of the most powerful ideas in engineering.

Different systems can safely share **physical infrastructure**

provided each system maintains its own independent operation.

Examples include:

- Internet cables
- Electrical power grids
- USB devices
- Ethernet networks
- Computer buses
- Fiber-optic communication

Sharing infrastructure reduces:

- Cost
- Complexity
- Material usage

without reducing functionality.

---

## Hidden Lesson

Petzold isn't simply teaching telegraphs.

He is introducing a universal engineering principle:

> **Share resources whenever possible without sacrificing independence.**

This philosophy appears repeatedly throughout computer science,

including:

- Operating systems
- Cloud computing
- Databases
- Multiprocessor systems
- Networking

# 20. Size Matters

Electrical resistance depends mainly upon two things:

- The material
- The cross-sectional area

Earth is not made of highly conductive material,

Compare:

```text

Diameter:

≈2 mm
```

with

```text
Earth

Diameter:

≈12,742 km
```

The Earth provides **billions of parallel paths** through which electrical current can travel.

## Engineering Insight

Imagine squeezing people through:

```text

```


Even if each doorway is small,

# 22. Ground vs Common

This is one of the most misunderstood concepts in electronics.

Although the symbols often look similar,

**Ground** and **Common** are not the same thing.

---

## Common

A **Common** is simply a point shared by multiple electrical circuits.

Example:

```text
Battery (-)

──────────────
```

A common **does not require any connection to Earth**.

Most electronic devices—

including computers—

contain numerous common connections that never touch the ground outside the device.

---

## Ground

Ground means a **physical electrical connection to the Earth**.

```text
Battery

↓

Copper Rod

↓

Earth
```

Ground is literally the planet beneath your feet.

---

## Comparison

| Common | Ground |
| ------- | ------ |
| Shared electrical reference | Physical connection to Earth |
| May never touch Earth | Always connected to Earth |
| Common inside computers | Used in power systems and safety |
| Used for circuit reference | Used as voltage reference and return path |

---

## Computer Science Connection

Inside a computer,

most circuits use a **common reference**,

not Earth ground.

Engineers often use the word **ground** informally,

but in many digital circuits,

it simply refers to the circuit's common reference point rather than an actual connection to Earth.

Understanding this distinction becomes increasingly important when studying electronics.

# 25. Why Don't Electrons Get Lost?

An obvious question arises.

Millions of electrical circuits use Earth simultaneously.

How do electrons know where to go?

The answer is simple.

They don't.

Electrons have no map.

They merely respond to local electric fields.

Current naturally follows the complete electrical circuit established by the voltage source.

---

## Engineering Insight

Think of a marble placed on a hill.

The marble doesn't know where to roll.

Gravity determines its motion.

Similarly,

electrons do not "choose" their path.

Voltage creates an electric field,

# 26. Why Doesn't This Work with Flashlight Batteries?

Suppose we connect a small flashlight battery to the Earth.

```text
Battery

3V
```

Assume the resistance through the Earth is approximately:

```text
100Ω
```

Using **Ohm's Law**:

```text
I = V / R

= 3 / 100

= 0.03A
```

The resulting current is extremely small.

It is far too weak to light a typical flashlight bulb.

---

Now imagine using a much larger voltage source.

```text
120V
```

Current becomes:

```text
I = 120 / 100

= 1.2A
```

This current is much larger.

Now the Earth becomes a practical return conductor.

---

## Why This Matters

Ground-return systems become practical only when sufficient voltage exists to overcome the resistance of the Earth.

This is why large telegraph systems and electrical power systems can successfully use Earth,

while a tiny flashlight battery generally cannot.

---

## Engineering Insight

Small batteries are designed for:

- Short distances
- Small loads
- Low current applications

Large communication and power systems require:

- Higher voltages
- Greater transmission distances
- Larger electrical loads

# 31. The Problem with Long Wires

Copper is an excellent conductor,

but it is **not a perfect conductor**.

Every wire has some resistance.

The longer the wire becomes,

the greater its resistance.

```text
Longer Wire

↓

More Resistance

↓

Less Current

↓

Weaker Signal
```

For short distances,

this isn't a serious problem.

However,

telegraph lines eventually stretched for hundreds of miles.

As resistance increased,

the current reaching the receiving station became weaker and weaker.

Eventually,

the receiving bulb (or later, the electromagnet) became too weak to operate.

The signal simply disappeared.

---

## Hidden Lesson

Nothing in engineering scales perfectly.

A design that works over **10 meters**

may completely fail over **1000 kilometers**.

Long-distance communication introduces entirely new engineering challenges.

# 32. Wire Gauge (AWG)

The thickness of electrical wire is measured using:

```text
American Wire Gauge

(AWG)
```

The numbering system is surprisingly opposite to what many beginners expect.

```text
Smaller Number

=

Thicker Wire
```

Example:

```text
10 AWG

↓

Thicker
```

```text
20 AWG

↓

Thinner
```

Thicker wires have:

- Lower resistance
- Lower voltage loss
- Higher current capacity
- Less heating

Thinner wires have:

- Higher resistance
- Greater energy loss
- Lower current capacity

---

## Why This Matters

Choosing the correct wire thickness is one of the most important engineering decisions.

A wire that is too thin:

- Wastes energy
- Produces excessive heat
- Limits current flow

A wire that is too thick:

- Costs more
- Is heavier
- May be unnecessary

Engineering is always a balance between **performance** and **cost**.

---

# 34. Two Engineering Solutions

Engineers discovered two practical solutions.

---

## Solution 1 — Use Thicker Wire

Increasing wire thickness reduces resistance.

```text
Thicker Wire

↓

Lower Resistance

↓

More Current
```

Unfortunately,

thicker copper wire is much more expensive.

For thousands of miles of telegraph line,

this solution quickly becomes impractical.

---

## Solution 2 — Increase Voltage

Instead of increasing wire size,

engineers can increase the voltage.

Suppose a system must deliver:

```text
100 Watts
```

At:

```text
10V
```

Current becomes:

```text
10A
```

Now increase the voltage.

```text
100V
```

Current becomes:

```text
1A
```

For the same amount of power,

higher voltage requires much lower current.

Lower current dramatically reduces transmission losses.

---

## Computer Science Connection

This exact principle is still used today.

Electricity generated at power stations is transmitted using:

- Hundreds of kilovolts
- Extremely high voltage
- Relatively low current

Only near homes is the voltage reduced to safer levels.

The same engineering principle discovered during the telegraph era now powers the entire electrical grid.

# 36. The Device That Changed Everything

Petzold ends the chapter with an intriguing statement:

```text

```

A relay receives a weak electrical signal,

The new circuit generates a fresh,

strong electrical signal.

Instead of attempting to send one weak signal across thousands of miles,

telegraph stations simply regenerated the signal every few hundred miles.

Long-distance communication suddenly became practical.

This simple idea transformed communication forever.

# Common Mistakes Beginners Make

- [ ] **Thinking the telegraph invented Morse Code.**
  - **Truth:** Morse Code already existed as a communication code. The telegraph simply provided a faster way to transmit it over long distances.

- [ ] **Thinking electricity carries words or letters.**
  - **Truth:** Electricity carries only electrical signals. The receiver interprets those signals as Morse Code, which is then decoded into letters.

- [ ] **Thinking Common and Ground are the same thing.**
  - **Truth:** A **Common** is a shared electrical reference inside a circuit. A **Ground** is a physical electrical connection to the Earth.

- [ ] **Thinking Earth is an excellent conductor.**
  - **Truth:** Earth is actually a poor conductor compared to copper. It works because it is unimaginably large, providing countless parallel conductive paths.

- [ ] **Thinking electrons know where to go.**
  - **Truth:** Electrons have no intelligence or destination. They simply respond to electric fields created by voltage differences.

- [ ] **Thinking higher voltage is always more dangerous because it creates more current.**
  - **Truth:** Voltage provides the electrical "push." The actual current depends on both voltage and resistance, according to Ohm's Law.

- [ ] **Thinking thicker wires carry electricity faster.**
  - **Truth:** Thicker wires mainly reduce resistance and energy loss. They allow more current to flow efficiently but do not significantly change the speed at which electrical signals propagate.

- [ ] **Thinking long wires fail because electricity slows down.**
  - **Truth:** Signals weaken because resistance causes voltage drop and energy loss—not because electricity suddenly moves slowly.

- [ ] **Thinking the relay amplifies electricity.**
  - **Truth:** A relay does not amplify a signal. It uses a weak signal to operate a completely new circuit, creating a fresh, strong signal.

- [ ] **Thinking computers were invented directly from calculators.**
  - **Truth:** This chapter shows that computers evolved from solving communication problems. Telegraphs led to relays, relays led to logic, and logic led to computers.
